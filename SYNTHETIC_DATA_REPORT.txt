SYNTHETIC DATA REPORT
Reciprocal matching project (DS340)
Written 2026-10-31

This report records how the synthetic users were made, what was tested along the
way, what changed as a result, and where the final dataset stands.


1. SUMMARY
----------
The final dataset is 2,000 simulated Boston University students. Each has a public
profile and a hidden written persona. A persona is 2 to 3 sentences written by Claude
Haiku 5.5 from the user's profile and five random personality traits, and it describes
what the person is like and what they want in a partner.

Simulated users rate profiles through TypeSafe's Jev model. Jev answers one yes/no
question, "would this person like this profile?", and returns a probability from 0 to
1. That probability is the saved rating.

Written personas replaced the original trait-score personas because Jev barely
responded to trait scores. In the final test, with each user rating as themselves:

    Measure                          Trait numbers    Written personas
    Rater x target interaction       0.115            0.191
    Noise (repeating a rating)       0.011            0.014
    Mean like probability            0.585            0.307

The rater x target interaction measures whether different people like different
targets. That is the hidden taste a matching algorithm has to learn.

Main caution: all of this is Jev's estimate of how invented people would react. It
is not measured human behavior. See section 7.


2. THE ORIGINAL DATASET (10,000 USERS)
--------------------------------------
Profiles (scripts/generate_profiles.py, Data Generation/synthetic_data.py, seed 42).
Each value is drawn uniformly unless noted.

    gender          man or woman, 50/50 (5,027 men and 4,973 women)
    name            80 names per gender
    age             18 to 24
    height_cm       150 to 200, for all genders
    religion        10 options
    school          always BU
    major           42 options
    job             8 student jobs
    interests       1 to 3 from 383 unique hobbies (hobbylist.csv)
    clubs           0 to 3 of 432 BU organizations, leaving out clubs limited to
                    the other gender
    interested_in   own gender 3% of the time, otherwise the other gender
                    (296 users, or 2.96%, were interested in their own gender)
    looking_for     short-term, long-term, or just a date
    agent           always True

Fields are drawn independently, apart from name, clubs, and interested_in depending
on gender.

Personas, version 1 (Data Generation/synthetic_personas.py, now superseded). Each user
got 25 trait scores in 6 categories, drawn from a normal distribution with mean 0.5 and
SD 0.2, rounded to two decimals. Out-of-range draws were redrawn. Regenerating the data
in memory reproduced every profile and persona exactly.

Storage: profiles in main.db, personas in a separate personas.db, so that the matching
algorithm has no route to hidden data.


3. WHAT WE TESTED AND WHAT WE CHANGED
-------------------------------------
Sample sizes in these tests are small. They were chosen to guide decisions cheaply, so
treat the numbers as rough sizes. The Jev question was also reworded between tests 3
and 4, and again before test 5, so compare across tests loosely.

Test 1: 10-level score, trait-score personas (100 ratings)
    Jev rated profiles on a 1 to 10 scale using a Score question.
    Result: scores ran 3.38 to 7.49, mean 5.92, SD 1.03. Incompatible profiles scored
    1.39 in one check, and there were no failures.
    Problem: for one rater, changing the persona moved scores by SD 0.15, while
    changing the target moved them by SD 1.04.
    Also seen: Jev under-used the "Neutral" level (mean probability 0.065, below both
    neighbors).

Idea considered: z-score each label across ratings
    This was proposed to remove label bias. It wasn't used, since rare levels turned
    into noise (z-scores reached 5.7) and the results no longer summed to 1. The switch
    to a single label in test 2 removed the problem.

Test 2: one "like" label against the 10-level score (same 100 pairs)
    Jev's Noul question returns the probability of a yes.
    Result: correlation of 0.96 with the 10-level score. Range 0.34 to 0.76, with 81%
    of ratings between 0.3 and 0.7. An incompatible profile scored 0.05. Tokens per
    rating fell from 1,114 to 986.
    Decision: switched to the single label. Real people will like or pass rather than
    give a 1 to 10 score, so training labels match test labels, and a pair's chance of
    matching is the product of their two probabilities.
    Persona effect was still barely above noise (SD about 0.026 against 0.015).

Test 3: are personas the problem? (notebooks/persona_test.ipynb, 384 ratings)
    Hypotheses tested: Jev itself is the limit, Gaussian traits have too little
    variation, or personas need to fit their profiles. 8 raters, one target each.

        Condition                               SD of like probability
        Repeating the same request (noise)      0.010
        Gaussian traits                         0.027
        Uniform traits                          0.043
        Traits consistent with the profile      0.024
        Extreme traits (all 0 or 1)             0.037
        Different target, same persona          0.080

    Opposite written preferences about religion moved scores far more:

        Written preference                 Religious target    Atheist target
        Only wants someone very religious  0.46                0.03
        Only wants someone not religious   0.06                0.83

    Conclusions: Jev can use persona information, so Jev alone isn't the limit. Uniform
    helped modestly. More numeric variation doesn't close the gap, since extreme traits
    did no better than uniform. Consistency with the profile showed no gain. The weak
    link was the trait-number format.

Test 4: Claude-written personas (notebooks/written_personas.ipynb, 792 ratings)
    50 personas were written from five uniform traits, without seeing a profile. 4
    raters, each rating 10 personas against 6 targets in every condition.

        Measure                     Gaussian    Uniform    Written
        SD across personas          0.034       0.044      0.166
        Persona x target            0.011       0.015      0.144
        Noise                       0.010       0.010      0.015
        Ratings at 0.3 or below     5%          6%         40%
        Ratings at 0.7 or above     25%         18%        17%

    Sense check on 3 raters: high-traditionalism personas favored religious targets by
    0.15, and low-traditionalism personas favored non-religious ones by 0.09.
    Decision: use written personas, and show Claude the profile so that a persona can't
    conflict with it.

Test 5: pilot of 50 personas written with the profile (264 ratings)
    Same persona-swap design as test 4: SD across personas 0.176, persona x target
    0.149, target 0.080, noise 0.016. The signal held, so the full set was generated.

Test 6: final check (notebooks/final_dataset_check.ipynb, 1,080 ratings)
    See section 5.


4. THE FINAL DATASET (2,000 USERS)
----------------------------------
Generator: Data Generation/written_personas.py.

Users: a seeded random sample (seed 42) of the original 10,000 profiles, unchanged and
with the same IDs. A smaller pool means more pairs rate each other for the same number
of ratings.

    Men interested in women      989
    Women interested in men      961
    Men interested in men         29
    Women interested in women     21

    Looking for: 711 short-term, 680 just a date, 609 long-term

Traits: five traits drawn uniformly from 0 to 1 for each user, from the seed and user
ID: extraversion, openness, conscientiousness, traditionalism, achievement drive.

Writing: Claude Haiku 5.5 received the profile (without name, ID, or agent flag) and the
traits. It was asked for 2 to 3 sentences in the third person covering what the person
is like and what they want and don't want in a partner, using only things a dating
profile shows.

Result:
    2,000 personas written with 0 failures
    385 to 950 characters, mean 590
    2 sentences: 919, 3 sentences: 1,076, 4 sentences: 5
    No duplicates. Mean word overlap between two personas is 0.22.

Storage:
    data/main.db        Profiles, ratings, and runs. Public to the matcher.
    data/personas.db    Personas and rating transcripts. Hidden from the matcher.

generation_seeds on each persona records the seed, model, and five traits.
paths.py is the one place that says where the current dataset lives.

The original 10,000-user main.db and personas.db were left in place in the project root.

Rating: raters.py sends the rater's profile, written persona, and the target's profile
to jev-1.14.0. The version is pinned so that a new release can't change scores partway
through an experiment. User IDs and the agent flag are never sent. The question is:

    `rater` is a college student using a dating app, and `rater_persona` says what
    they're like and what they want in a partner. Judging as this person would, and
    following the preferences in `rater_persona`, would `rater` like `shown_profile`?
    `rater.interested_in` says who they're attracted to, so anyone outside it is a no.


5. FINAL RESULTS
----------------
Data checks on all 2,000 users:
    Users without a persona                 0
    Personas without a user                 0
    Duplicate personas                      0
    Profiles changed from the original      0
    Users marked as agents                  2,000
    Sampled persona text in main.db         0 (25 personas checked against profiles)

Jev test: 20 men and 20 women each rated 12 targets with their own profile, once with
their written persona and once with their original trait numbers.

    Measure                          Trait numbers    Written personas
    Rater x target interaction       0.115            0.191
    Rater effect                     0.060            0.110
    Target effect                    0.033            0.071
    Noise                            0.011            0.014
    Mean like probability            0.585            0.307
    SD of like probability           0.132            0.220
    Ratings at 0.3 or below          5%               60%
    Ratings at 0.7 or above          27%              8%

The interaction is about 14 times noise. Trait numbers already reached 0.115 here,
because raters' own profiles differ and Jev reads them. So written personas add about
1.7 times on top of that. The near 4-fold gain in test 4 only applies when the persona
alone changes.

Do the preferences make sense? Mean like probability with written personas:
    Same relationship goal    0.451 against 0.239 (difference 0.21)
    Same religion             0.405 against 0.297 (difference 0.11)

    Traditionalism    Religious target    Non-religious target    Difference
    Low               0.325               0.418                   -0.09
    Middle            0.305               0.372                   -0.07
    High              0.270               0.229                   +0.04

The match differences could partly come from Jev reading both profiles. The
traditionalism gap moves the expected way but is weak, and weaker than in test 4.

End-to-end run: a real 100-step simulation through the framework, using
CompatibleRandomMatcher and Jev, saved as jev-trial-final in data/main.db.
    0 failures, mean 0.33, SD 0.22, range 0.03 to 0.80, all between compatible users
    0.14 seconds per step, 753 tokens per rating

The full 100,000-rating run is reported in section 6.

Cost:
    Writing the 2,000 personas     $0.27
    All tests and trials           about $0.15
    100,000-rating baseline        $3.16


6. THE 100,000-RATING RANDOM BASELINE
-------------------------------------
Run ID: random-baseline-100k. CompatibleRandomMatcher showed each user random compatible
profiles, and Jev rated them using each user's written persona. This is the baseline a
real matching algorithm has to beat. Details and charts are in
notebooks/random_baseline_metrics.ipynb.

The run:
    Steps attempted               100,000
    Ratings saved                 99,999
    Failed steps                  1 (a brief server error from Jev, at step 26,802)
    Pairs rated twice             0
    Ratings with a transcript     99,999
    Ratings per user              Median 50 given and 50 received
    Cost                          $3.16 (752 input tokens per rating)

    The first 1,646 ratings ran one at a time. The run was then stopped and resumed
    with 8 parallel workers, which raised speed from about 7 to about 44 ratings a
    second. The matcher doesn't learn, so this shouldn't affect the results.

    All 50 same-gender users rated everyone compatible and dropped out of the pool.

Results:
    Mean like probability                  0.324 (95% interval 0.318 to 0.330)
    SD of like probability                 0.232
    Ratings at 0.3 or below                56%
    Ratings at 0.5 or above                27%
    Ratings at 0.7 or above                9%
    Pairs who rated each other             3,104 (6% of ratings are in such a pair)
    Expected matches                       414, or 4.14 per 1,000 ratings
    Match chance per pair                  0.133 (95% interval 0.128 to 0.139)
    Pairs with both at 0.5 or above        415
    Pairs with both at 0.7 or above        62
    Correlation of scores within a pair    0.47
    Learning curve per 5,000 ratings       Flat, 0.314 to 0.330

Expected matches is the main number to compare against. A pair's match chance is the
product of their two scores, which assumes each decision is independent once the
probabilities are known. One seeded draw of simulated swipes gave 427 matches.

What drives ratings (simple averages, not causal effects):
    Same relationship goal: 0.455 against 0.258 (difference 0.20)
    Same religion:          0.412 against 0.315 (difference 0.10)
    Spread across raters:   SD 0.14
    Spread across targets:  SD 0.07

    Mean like probability, by rater's goal (rows) and target's goal (columns):
                     short-term    long-term    just a date
    short-term       0.446         0.163        0.453
    long-term        0.091         0.422        0.114
    just a date      0.422         0.261        0.492

7. HOW TO REGENERATE
--------------------
Requires the TYPESAFE_API_KEY and ANTHROPIC_API_KEY environment variables.

    python scripts/generate_profiles.py                 Original 10,000 profiles
    python "Data Generation/synthetic_personas.py"      Original trait personas
    python "Data Generation/written_personas.py"        The final 2,000 users
    python scripts/run_experiment.py --run-id NEW_NAME --steps 100000

The profiles, the sample of users, and the traits repeat exactly for the same seeds.
Claude's wording does NOT repeat, so regenerating gives different personas.

The ratings won't repeat exactly either, because Jev's answers vary slightly and each
rerun costs money. If a run stops, the same command continues it from its last saved rating.

Databases and backups are excluded from Git by .gitignore, so they exist only on this
computer unless copied elsewhere. Copy them, because the exact personas can't be recreated
from the code. The ratings export has no persona text and can be committed.


8. LIMITATIONS
--------------
- Simulated behavior: ratings are Jev's estimates about invented people. They haven't
  been compared with real people's choices.
- Low like rates: written personas are picky, with a mean like probability of 0.31 and
  60% of ratings at 0.3 or below. Mutual matches will be rare.
- Unrealistic profiles: most fields are independent and uniform. Each religion is about
  10% of users, height doesn't vary by gender, and majors don't affect jobs or clubs.
  They don't reflect BU's actual students.
- Same-gender pool: only 50 of the 2,000 users are interested in their own gender, so
  results for them will be thin.
- One gender of interest: interested_in holds one value, so nobody is interested in both
  men and women.
- Small tests: the final test used 40 raters and 24 targets, across two groups.
- Uneven final comparison: the Jev question no longer explains the trait-number scale,
  which may slightly weaken the trait-number result.
- Writing quality: 344 personas (17%) use gendered words despite being told not to. An
  automated check flagged 7 possible conflicts with interested_in, and on reading them
  none was real (they were club names or self-descriptions). The check only looks at
  specific words, so it can't rule out every conflict. About 30 personas use trait words
  like "openness", and some name very specific clubs that few profiles could match.
- Persona and profile overlap: personas now reflect profiles, so a matcher can partly
  infer tastes from public data. This is realistic, but it means persona and profile
  effects can't be cleanly separated.
- Not repeatable word for word: repeated Jev ratings vary by about 0.01 to 0.02, so seeds
  repeat who sees whom but not exact scores.


9. WHAT IS STILL OPEN
---------------------
- The real matching algorithm hasn't been built. CompatibleRandomMatcher is the
  baseline it has to beat, at 4.14 expected matches per 1,000 ratings.
- A matcher that learns from each rating should run one at a time or in small batches,
  so comparing it with this baseline may take much longer than this run did.
- No real people are in the data yet. The test set needs its own separate database.
