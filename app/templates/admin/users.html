Saved data: the ground truth

Every score is stored in the database as it's given, so nothing here is lost when the page closes. To compare a model's predictions with what people actually thought, download the ground truth. Everyone in it has a participant id: a salted hash, never a name or email, stable for as long as EXPORT_SALT stays the same, and the same id the impressions export uses.

  • participants.csv: participant id, gender, age band, and how many ratings each gave and received.
  • scores.csv: one row per rating, with rater id, target id, score from 0 to 1, and when.
  • matrix.csv: the whole grid of who likes whom: one row per rater, one column per person rated, blank where nobody has rated yet.
  • README.txt: what every column means, and how to compare a model with it.

Interest

Each row is a rater, and each column is the person they rated. Darker means more interested. Hatched squares haven't been rated yet. People are grouped by gender, then ordered by the mean score they received, so the most liked people in each group come first. Hover over a square for details.

Reciprocal interest

For each pair, the sum of both people's scores for each other, from 0 to 2. It's filled in only where both have rated each other, so the grid is symmetric. Dark squares are pairs who both liked each other.

What goes with interest

Mean score for each value of a profile field, or of how two people compare. Charts with a line per rater gender show whether women and men respond differently. r is the Pearson correlation with the score, from −1 to 1. η² is the share of the variation in scores explained by the category, from 0 to 1. These are descriptive: one person's ratings are related to each other, so they aren't significance tests.

How these numbers are calculated

Everything uses each rating once, from approved participants who haven't opted out. Write sij for person i's score for person j.

Scores

s = (rating − 1) / 9

People rate from 1 to 10, and the score is stored from 0 to 1, the scale the simulated agents' ratings use. A score counts as liked when s ≥ t, where t = (POSITIVE_THRESHOLD − 1) / 9 = {{ threshold|round(3) }}.

Heat maps

interest cell (i, j) = sij
reciprocal cell (i, j) = sij + sji, only when both exist, so 0 ≤ cell ≤ 2

Shade is proportional to the value, from paper (0) to ink (1, or 2 for reciprocal). Within each gender, people are ordered by the mean score they received, s̄j = (1 / nj) Σi sij. A pair "both liked each other" when sij ≥ t and sji ≥ t.

Distributions and the liking table

share in bin b = (number of the group's scores with b/10 ≤ s < (b+1)/10) / (number of the group's scores)
share liked = (number of ratings with s ≥ t) / (number of ratings), for each rater group and rated group

A score of exactly 1.0 goes in the last bin. "Given" groups ratings by the rater's gender, and "received" by the rated person's gender.

Mean score per category

ȳg = (1 / ng) Σ s, over the ng ratings in category g

Numbers like height are grouped into bins only for the charts. The statistics below use the raw numbers.

Pearson correlation, r

r = Σk (xk − x̄)(yk − ȳ) / √[ Σk (xk − x̄)² · Σk (yk − ȳ)² ]

Each rating k is one point: yk is its score, and xk is the rated person's height in cm or age in years, the height difference (rated − rater, in cm), or the age gap |rated − rater| in years. r runs from −1 to 1, and 0 means no straight-line relationship. It's left out with fewer than 3 ratings, or when x or y doesn't vary.

Regression slope

ŷ = a + bx,   b = Σk (xk − x̄)(yk − ȳ) / Σk (xk − x̄)²,   a = ȳ − bx̄

This is ordinary least squares with one predictor, over the same points as r. The slope b is the change in mean score per cm or per year, so "slope +0.0040 per cm" means 10 cm taller goes with a score 0.04 higher. Its R², the share of variance the line explains, equals r². Equivalently, b = r · (SD of y / SD of x).

Correlation ratio, η²

η² = Σg ng (ȳg − ȳ)² / Σk (yk − ȳ)²

For categories like major or job: the share of the variation in scores explained by which category the rated person is in, from 0 (every category has the same mean) to 1. It equals the R² of a least-squares regression of the score on one indicator variable per category, which is a one-way ANOVA. It uses every category, before rare ones are pooled into "other" for the chart. Interests aren't given η², because people list several and the groups overlap, so each is compared with the overall mean instead.

What these numbers don't tell you

Each person gives many ratings, and generous raters raise every score they give, so ratings aren't independent. That's why there are no p-values or confidence intervals here: treating every rating as independent would make them look far more certain than they are. Use these numbers to explore, and the export, with its stable pseudonyms, for proper models, such as mixed models with rater and rated person as random effects.
